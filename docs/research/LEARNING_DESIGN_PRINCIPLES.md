# Educational Design — The Skill Should Emerge From Play

**Status:** ORIGINAL PRODUCT/DESIGN PROPOSAL. This document is a design rationale, **not** a literature review proving learning outcomes.

## Core promise

Players enjoy searching for caches, camps, bridges, trails and hidden points. The rules quietly teach coordinate order, area resolution, reverse reading, map legends and eventually distance/direction.

The game should **never mislead the player** about what they learned: a fictional training grid can simulate plotting practice, but is not a real GPS navigator and not military qualification.

## Design loop

1. **Show a believable problem:** "Three supply crates are on this map" rather than "Answer this geography test."
2. **Require a real action:** read the grid, plot a cell, reverse-find a reference or interpret a coherent spatial clue.
3. **Respond with useful feedback:** show which easting/northing or sub-square was misread, instead of vague "incorrect."
4. **Repeat with variation:** different landmarks, distractors and maps; keep the same coordinate conventions.
5. **Remove scaffolding gradually:** start with highlighted axis order, then optional hints, then mixed unaided missions.
6. **Mix modes to reveal transfer:** after plotting a reference, ask for the grid of a feature on the same map; later combine with a distance/landmark clue.
7. **Keep delight proportional:** subtle stamps, discovery journal and optional celebrations; avoid reward mechanics that mask an unfun puzzle.

## Guardrails

- **Do not fake precision** by demanding one pixel in a 1 km or 100 m square.
- **Do not punish ordinary touch error** as intellectual failure: allow pan, zoom, undo, and controlled reticle.
- **Do not imply all symbols are universal**: use a legend; align feature and clue data.
- **Do not give away all answers in the UI**: if the map labels every exact object, the exercise may reduce to tapping rather than reading.
- **Do not require a timer** for a casual puzzle by default.
- **Do not overload chapters**: introduce one skill, practice it, revisit with varied targets, then combine it.
- **Do not overbuild artwork or physics**: first ship a coherent illustrated map and verified grid transformations.

## Evidence we'd eventually want

- **Gameplay usability:** can a new player complete an early four-digit hunt and explain right/up without external tutoring?
- **Retention test:** after playing mixed chapters, can they identify a new square and generate a reverse reference without on-screen coaching?
- **Transfer boundary:** optional paper-map practice exercise with equivalent math; do not claim an outdoor land-navigation transfer until evaluated.
- **Fairness:** count error rates for tiny target selection vs actual grid-order misunderstandings separately.

## Proposed initial fun-vs-learning balance

Early chapter: 80% target-hunting and satisfying feedback, 20% mini-explanations. Later chapter: same core fun, but more independent judgement, mixed objectives and reduced hints. Percentages describe **creative intent**, not a validated pedagogical metric.
